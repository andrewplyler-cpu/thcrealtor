STR Investing in NC High Country: Honest Analysis

Andrew Plyler, REALTOR® · November 24, 2026 · Boone, NC

With Thanksgiving just days away and the holiday season ramping up fast, the High Country is about to do what it does best — fill up. Valle Crucis will be packed with families cutting Christmas trees at Mistletoe Meadows, the slopes at Sugar Mountain and Beech Mountain will be praying for a cold snap, and downtown Boone will buzz with App State students, returning alumni, and visitors stocking up at the Mast General Store on King Street. For people watching all of this from the outside, it raises an obvious question: should I buy a short-term rental up here?

As someone who grew up coming to this area, went to school at Appalachian State, and made the High Country my permanent home in 2020, I want to give you the honest answer — not the glossy investor pitch, and not the reflexive doom-and-gloom either. The truth, as usual, lives somewhere in the middle.

Why the High Country Attracts STR Investors

The demand case for short-term rentals in Watauga, Avery, and Ashe counties is genuinely strong. The region draws visitors across all four seasons: leaf-peepers and hikers in fall, skiers and holiday travelers in winter, waterfall-chasers and motorcyclists in spring, and families fleeing the Carolina heat all summer long. That four-season appeal is relatively rare in North Carolina and gives High Country rentals a longer potential earning window than many coastal markets that go quiet after Labor Day.

Boone NC real estate has also appreciated meaningfully over the past several years, which means buyers are not just looking at rental income — they're looking at an asset that has historically held and grown its value. For families considering a second home that can pay some of its own way, that combination is appealing. And with Appalachian State University pulling in a consistent stream of parents, prospective students, and event visitors, there is a demand floor that most purely resort markets don't have.

What the Numbers Actually Look Like (And What They Don't Tell You)

I'm not going to invent occupancy rates or average nightly figures for you. What I will tell you is that performance varies enormously depending on property type, location within the county, amenities, and how well the property is managed. A well-appointed cabin with a hot tub and mountain views on the Banner Elk side of Avery County is going to perform very differently from a dated condo on the outskirts of Boone with no outdoor living space.

What the gross revenue projections on listing sites often leave out are the real costs: HOA fees (which in some communities specifically restrict or heavily regulate STRs), property management fees typically running 20–30% of gross revenue, cleaning costs, maintenance reserves, insurance premiums that are meaningfully higher for short-term rentals, and the ongoing capital investment required to keep a property competitive in an increasingly crowded market. When you stack those against gross revenue, net returns can look quite different from the headline number.

That said, strong performers do exist. Properties that check the right boxes — hot tub, game room, reliable high-speed internet, good views, and professional photography — consistently outperform the average. The market rewards investment in the product.

County-by-County: Watauga, Avery, and Ashe

Each county has its own character and its own STR landscape. Here's a quick honest read on each:

  • Watauga County is anchored by Boone and Blowing Rock, and benefits from the Appalachian State housing demand, four-season tourism, and strong name recognition. Zoning and short-term rental regulations here are worth examining carefully — the regulatory environment has been evolving, and what was permissible a few years ago may face more scrutiny now.
  • Avery County is the ski country hub, with Banner Elk, Beech Mountain, and Sugar Mountain drawing a heavily winter-weighted crowd. STR supply here is high, which means differentiation matters more than ever. Mountain property NC buyers in Avery should underwrite conservatively for shoulder seasons.
  • Ashe County is the most underappreciated of the three. With lower price points, beautiful pastoral scenery along the New River, and a growing tourism profile, it offers real opportunity — particularly for investors who get in front of the curve rather than chasing it.

The Regulatory Landscape Is Shifting — Pay Attention

This may be the most important thing I can tell you right now: short-term rental regulations across Western North Carolina are in active flux. Several municipalities and counties are revisiting their zoning codes in response to community pressure around housing availability and neighborhood character. Before you buy any property with STR intent, you need to verify current regulations at the county and municipal level, review any HOA documents with the same scrutiny, and think carefully about what happens to your investment thesis if the rules change. A good High Country REALTOR will help you ask the right questions before you're under contract, not after.

So Should You Buy an STR in the High Country?

For the right buyer, with realistic expectations, the right property, and a long-term orientation — yes, the High Country is a compelling STR market. The demand drivers are real, the lifestyle upside of owning mountain property NC is real, and the asset quality tends to hold up well. But treat it like a business from day one. Underwrite conservatively, budget for real costs, understand the regulatory environment, and don't let an optimistic pro forma substitute for honest due diligence.
